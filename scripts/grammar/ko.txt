=== ko.particle_topic_eun_neun | topic marker 은/는
## What it is
은/는 marks the topic: what the sentence is about, "as for X…". It often carries a sense of contrast with other things that could have been said ("as for me, at least…").

## How to make it
- After a consonant: 은. 사람은, 책은
- After a vowel: 는. 나는, 친구는
- Contractions in speech and lyrics: 나는 → 난, 너는 → 넌, 저는 → 전, 그건 (그것은), 이건 (이것은)
- It can follow other particles: 에서는, 에게는, 한테는, 까지는.

## When to use it
- Introducing or keeping the topic of what you're saying.
저는 학생이에요. — I'm a student.
- Contrast: this one, as opposed to others.
커피는 좋아하는데 차는 안 좋아해. — Coffee I like, but tea I don't.
- General statements and definitions.
고양이는 물을 싫어해. — Cats hate water.
- After another particle, to set that phrase up as the topic or contrast it.
학교에서는 조용해. — At school, at least, he's quiet.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- New information, or answering "who / what?": use 이/가. 누가 왔어? — 민수가 왔어. (not 민수는)
- The subject inside a describing clause before a noun takes 이/가. 내가 산 책 — the book I bought (not 나는 산 책)
- "I have / I like / I need X" puts 이/가 on X: 나는 시간이 없어. — I don't have time.

## Nuance and register
Neutral in every speech level. Heavy contrast can sound pointed: 이건 괜찮아 can imply "but the others aren't".

## Common mistakes
- Using 은/는 for brand-new information in a question's answer; that's 이/가.
- Forgetting that 난 / 넌 / 전 are just 나는 / 너는 / 저는.

=== ko.particle_subject_i_ga | subject marker 이/가
## What it is
이/가 marks the subject, singling it out: "it's X that…". It introduces new information and answers "who?" or "what?".

## How to make it
- After a consonant: 이. 사람이, 책이
- After a vowel: 가. 친구가, 의자가
- Special forms: 나 + 가 → 내가, 너 + 가 → 네가 (often said and written 니가 in speech and lyrics), 저 + 가 → 제가, 누구 + 가 → 누가.

## When to use it
- New information or an answer to "who / what?".
누가 했어? — 내가 했어. — Who did it? — I did.
- Describing what happens or exists.
비가 와. — It's raining.
- With 있다 / 없다, 좋다, 싫다, 필요하다, 아프다: the thing had, liked, needed or hurting takes 이/가.
머리가 아파. — My head hurts.
- With 되다 and 아니다: what someone becomes or isn't.
의사가 됐어. — She became a doctor. 그건 내 잘못이 아니야. — That's not my fault.
- The subject inside a clause that describes a noun.
네가 좋아하는 노래 — the song you like

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Setting the topic or contrasting: use 은/는 (see that guide).
- The object of an action verb takes 을/를: 밥을 먹어 (not 밥이 먹어).

## Nuance and register
Neutral. Stressing 이/가 in an answer is like stressing the word in English: "*I* did it."

## Common mistakes
- 나가 / 너가 instead of 내가 / 네가 (너가 is common in casual speech, but 네가 is standard).
- Using 을/를 with 좋다: 너를 좋아해 (좋아하다, verb) but 네가 좋아 (좋다, adjective).

=== ko.particle_object_eul_reul | object marker 을/를
## What it is
을/를 marks the direct object: the thing an action is done to. 밥을 먹다 — eat rice; 너를 사랑해 — I love you.

## How to make it
- After a consonant: 을. 책을, 웃음을
- After a vowel: 를. 너를, 노래를
- In speech and lyrics 를 often shrinks to ㄹ on a vowel: 나를 → 날, 너를 → 널, 이것을 → 이걸, 그것을 → 그걸, 뭐를 → 뭘.
- It is often dropped in casual speech: 밥 먹었어? — Have you eaten?

## When to use it
- The object of an action verb.
영화를 봤어. — I watched a movie.
- After 하다 nouns to put the action in focus: 공부를 하다 = 공부하다.
- Sometimes to mark the destination with 가다 / 오다 in speech, or the route crossed: 길을 건너다 — cross the road.
- For emphasis on a word that wouldn't normally take it: 나를 좀 봐 sounds more insistent than 나 좀 봐.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Adjectives and "have / like / need" adjectives (좋다, 싫다, 있다, 필요하다) take 이/가, not 을/를: 네가 좋아.
- 좋아하다 / 싫어하다 (the verbs) do take 을/를: 너를 좋아해.
- 되다 takes 이/가: 가수가 되고 싶어.
- Meeting someone: 친구를 만나다 (을/를), but "resemble" is 닮다 with 을/를 or 와/과 — check each verb.

## Nuance and register
Neutral. Dropping it is casual; keeping it adds weight or clarity.

## Common mistakes
- 을/를 on the thing you like with the adjective 좋다.
- Not recognising 날 / 널 / 이걸 as 나를 / 너를 / 이것을.

=== ko.particle_ege_hante | to (a person) 에게 / 한테 / 께
## What it is
에게 and 한테 mark the person (or animal) something goes to or is aimed at: "to / for someone". With passive and causative verbs they mark "by whom".

## How to make it
- Attach directly to the noun, whatever its last sound: 친구에게, 친구한테
- 에게 is neutral and also written; 한테 is spoken and casual; 께 is the honorific form (선생님께).
- Contractions: 나에게 → 내게, 너에게 → 네게, 저에게 → 제게.
- "From a person": 에게서 / 한테서 (내게서 = 나에게서, "from me").

## When to use it
- Giving, sending, saying, asking: the person on the receiving end.
엄마한테 전화했어. — I called Mom. 내게 말해 줘. — Tell me.
- Feelings or effects aimed at someone.
너에게 미안해. — I'm sorry to you.
- "By" someone, with passive or causative verbs.
선생님한테 혼났어. — I got told off by the teacher.
- "From" someone, with 에게서 / 한테서: 친구한테서 들었어. — I heard it from a friend.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- For places and things, use 에: 학교에 보내다 (send to school), not 학교에게.
- For organisations, 에 is usual: 회사에 연락하다.

## Nuance and register
에게: neutral, writing, songs. 한테: everyday speech. 께: polite, for people you honour.

## Common mistakes
- 에게 with places or things.
- Reading 내게 / 네게 as new words: they are 나에게 / 너에게.

=== ko.particle_e | at / to / in (place and time) 에
## What it is
에 marks a point: where something is or goes, and when something happens. "At / in / to / on".

## How to make it
- Attach directly: 집에, 학교에, 3시에, 아침에

## When to use it
- Where something exists, with 있다 / 없다 / 살다.
방에 고양이가 있어. — There's a cat in the room.
- Destination, with 가다 / 오다 / 도착하다.
서울에 가. — I'm going to Seoul.
- A point in time.
주말에 만나자. — Let's meet at the weekend.
- "Per": 하루에 두 번 — twice a day.
- A cause, with some verbs: 소리에 놀랐어. — I was startled by the noise.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Where an action takes place: use 에서. 카페에서 공부해. — I study at a café.
- Times with 오늘, 어제, 내일, 지금 take no 에: 오늘 가 (not 오늘에).
- People: use 에게 / 한테.

## Nuance and register
Neutral in every level.

## Common mistakes
- 에 for the place of an action (공원에 운동해 → 공원에서 운동해).

=== ko.particle_eseo | at / from (place of action) 에서
## What it is
에서 marks where an action happens ("at, in") and where movement starts ("from").

## How to make it
- Attach directly: 학교에서, 집에서. In speech it can shrink to 서 after 여기 / 거기 / 어디: 여기서, 어디서.

## When to use it
- The place of an action.
도서관에서 공부했어. — I studied at the library.
- A starting point.
한국에서 왔어요. — I'm from Korea (I came from Korea).
- With 부터 / 까지 for ranges: 서울에서 부산까지 — from Seoul to Busan.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Where something simply is (있다 / 없다 / 살다): 에. 집에 있어 (not 집에서 있어), though 집에서 살다 is also heard.
- "From a person": 에게서 / 한테서.

## Nuance and register
Neutral.

## Common mistakes
- 에 for the place of an action, or 에서 for where something exists.

=== ko.particle_do | also / even 도
## What it is
도 means "also, too", and in negative or extreme sentences "even". It replaces 은/는, 이/가 and 을/를.

## How to make it
- Attach directly: 나도, 이것도. It follows other particles: 에도, 에서도, 한테도.

## When to use it
- Also, too.
나도 갈래. — I'll go too.
- Not even, with a negative.
하나도 몰라. — I don't know a single thing.
- Even (something surprising).
아이도 할 수 있어. — Even a child can do it.
- After amounts, to stress how much: 세 시간도 기다렸어. — I waited a whole three hours.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Don't stack it with 은/는, 이/가, 을/를: 나도 (not 나는도).

## Nuance and register
Neutral.

## Common mistakes
- Keeping the other particle: 책을도 → 책도.

=== ko.particle_ui | possessive 의
## What it is
의 links two nouns: "X's Y", "the Y of X".

## How to make it
- Attach directly: 친구의 집. In speech 의 is usually pronounced 에.
- With pronouns it contracts: 나의 → 내, 너의 → 네 (often said 니 or 너의 in songs to be clear), 저의 → 제.
- In everyday speech it's often dropped between nouns: 친구 집.

## When to use it
- Possession and belonging.
내 친구 — my friend. 너의 미소 — your smile.
- "Of", in titles and written style.
사랑의 의미 — the meaning of love.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Between two simple nouns in speech, Koreans usually leave it out: 학교 친구, 우리 엄마 (not 우리의 엄마).

## Nuance and register
Using 의 everywhere sounds written or poetic; lyrics use it more than speech.

## Common mistakes
- Not recognising 내 / 네 / 제 as 나의 / 너의 / 저의.
- 나의 엄마 instead of the natural 우리 엄마.

=== ko.casual_banmal | casual speech (반말)
## What it is
반말 is the casual level of speech, used with close friends, family, people younger than you, children, and in songs and inner thoughts. The core form is the verb stem + -아/어 (or 해 for 하다) with no ending after it: 먹어, 가, 해.

## How to make it
- Stem's last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ: + 아. 가다 → 가, 보다 → 봐, 알다 → 알아
- Any other vowel: + 어. 먹다 → 먹어, 주다 → 줘, 마시다 → 마셔
- 하다 → 해 (원하다 → 원해, 사랑하다 → 사랑해)
- 이다 → 야 after a vowel, 이야 after a consonant (나야, 사람이야); 아니다 → 아니야
- The same form is a statement, a question (rising tone) or a command, depending on intonation: 가? (going?), 가! (go!).
- Other casual endings: -지 (shared knowledge, softening), -냐 / -니 (questions), -자 (let's), -네 (discovery), -거든 (because, you see), -잖아 (you know).

## When to use it
- Talking to friends, siblings, partners, children.
뭐 해? — What are you doing?
- Statements, questions and commands with one form.
나를 원해. — You want me. / 원해? — Do you want it? / 와! — Come!
- Songs and inner monologue, where it feels intimate and direct.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Strangers, older people, colleagues and customers: use -아요/어요 (해요).
- Formal announcements and news: -습니다 / -ㅂ니다.
- A request "for me" isn't 반말 by itself: that's -아/어 주다 (해 줘, see that guide), which can be casual or polite.

## Nuance and register
Close and warm with the right person; rude with the wrong one. Switching to 반말 mid-conversation can signal intimacy, or anger.

## Common mistakes
- Using 반말 with someone older or a stranger.
- Thinking every -어 줘 is "casual speech": the meaning comes from 주다.

=== ko.polite_ayo_eoyo | polite speech -아요/어요 (해요)
## What it is
-아요/어요 is everyday polite speech: polite but not stiff, used with strangers, colleagues, older people and anyone you're not close to. It's 반말 + 요.

## How to make it
- Take the casual form and add 요: 가 → 가요, 먹어 → 먹어요, 해 → 해요
- 이다: 예요 after a vowel (친구예요), 이에요 after a consonant (학생이에요); 아니다 → 아니에요
- Like 반말, one form serves as statement, question or request by intonation.

## When to use it
- Most everyday conversation with people you aren't close to.
어디 가요? — Where are you going?
- Polite requests (with 주다).
도와주세요. — Please help me.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Very formal settings (news, presentations, the army, customer announcements): -습니다.
- Close friends and family: 반말; 요 there feels distant.

## Nuance and register
The safe default with strangers. Adding 요 to almost anything (even 네요, 고요) softens it politely.

## Common mistakes
- 이에요 / 예요 mixed up: vowel → 예요, consonant → 이에요.

=== ko.formal_seumnida | formal speech -습니다/ㅂ니다
## What it is
-습니다 / -ㅂ니다 is the formal polite level: news, speeches, presentations, the military, staff to customers, and set phrases (감사합니다).

## How to make it
- Stem ending in a consonant: + 습니다. 먹다 → 먹습니다
- Stem ending in a vowel: + ㅂ니다. 가다 → 갑니다, 하다 → 합니다
- ㄹ-stems drop the ㄹ: 알다 → 압니다
- Questions: -습니까 / -ㅂ니까. 아십니까?
- Commands: -(으)십시오. Suggestions: -(으)ㅂ시다.

## When to use it
- Formal speech and fixed polite phrases.
감사합니다. — Thank you. 처음 뵙겠습니다. — Nice to meet you.
- Announcements and broadcasts.
열차가 도착합니다. — The train is arriving.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Everyday polite conversation: -아요/어요 is more natural; -습니다 all the time sounds stiff.

## Nuance and register
Formal and respectful; in songs it's rare and stands out (often for effect, or quoting).

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting the ㄹ drop (알습니다 → 압니다).

=== ko.past_at_eot | past tense -았/었-
## What it is
-았/었- puts a verb or adjective in the past: "did, was".

## How to make it
- Stem vowel ㅏ or ㅗ: -았-. 가다 → 갔어, 보다 → 봤어
- Other vowels: -었-. 먹다 → 먹었어, 주다 → 줬어
- 하다 → 했- (했어, 했어요, 했습니다)
- 이다 → 였다 after a vowel (친구였어), 이었다 after a consonant (학생이었어)
- Then any ending: 갔어, 갔어요, 갔습니다, 갔지, 갔잖아
- -았었/었었-: a past that no longer holds ("used to / had").

## When to use it
- Finished events.
어제 영화 봤어. — I watched a movie yesterday.
- Past states.
그때 행복했어. — I was happy then.
- Some verbs use the past for a present state: 결혼했어 (is married), 늦었어 (is late), 닮았어 (looks alike).

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Ongoing past actions: -고 있었어 (was doing).
- Describing a noun in the past uses -(으)ㄴ / -던: 어제 본 영화 (the movie I watched yesterday).

## Nuance and register
Works at every speech level.

## Common mistakes
- 하다 → 핬어 (wrong) instead of 했어.
- Using present for states that Korean puts in the past (결혼해 → 결혼했어 for "is married").

=== ko.aux_a_juda | doing something for someone -아/어 주다 (해 줘)
## What it is
-아/어 주다 adds "for someone": the action is done as a favour or benefit. In requests it means "please do it (for me)": 해 줘, 해 주세요.

## How to make it
- Casual form of the verb + 주다: 하다 → 해 주다, 가다 → 가 주다, 기다리다 → 기다려 주다
- Requests: 해 줘 (casual), 해 주세요 (polite), 해 주시겠어요? (very polite)
- Often written together in lyrics and chat: 구해줘, 말해줘, 돌려놔줘
- For someone you honour: -아/어 드리다 (도와 드릴게요 — I'll help you).

## When to use it
- Asking someone to do something.
나를 구해 줘. — Save me. 내게 말해 줘. — Tell me.
- Saying you'll do something for someone.
내가 해 줄게. — I'll do it for you.
- Thanking for a favour: 와 줘서 고마워. — Thanks for coming.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- A plain casual sentence without a favour: just 반말 (원해 — I want it; 해 — I do it / do it).
- Asking someone to do something for your superior: -아/어 드리다.

## Nuance and register
Softens commands: 기다려! (Wait!) vs 기다려 줘 (Wait for me, please). In songs, 줘 often turns a line into a plea.

## Common mistakes
- Reading 해 줘 as two separate ideas; the 줘 is the favour.
- Using 주다 when you're doing it for someone older: 드리다.

=== ko.negative_an | negation 안
## What it is
안 before a verb or adjective means "not / don't": the speaker chooses not to, or it simply isn't so.

## How to make it
- 안 + verb / adjective: 안 가, 안 먹어, 안 예뻐
- 하다 verbs with a noun: noun + 안 + 하다: 공부 안 해 (not 안 공부해). 하다 adjectives keep it in front: 안 피곤해.
- The longer form -지 않다 means the same (see that guide).

## When to use it
- Not doing, by choice or as a fact.
나 안 가. — I'm not going.
- Something isn't so.
안 아파. — It doesn't hurt.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Can't (inability): 못.
- Commands ("don't"): -지 마. 가지 마 — Don't go.
- "Isn't" with nouns: 아니다. 내 잘못이 아니야.

## Nuance and register
Casual and common in speech; -지 않다 is more written or careful.

## Common mistakes
- 안 공부해 instead of 공부 안 해.
- 안 for "don't" commands instead of -지 마.

=== ko.connective_aseo | because / and then -아서/어서
## What it is
-아서/어서 links two clauses: the first is the reason for the second ("so"), or happens first and leads into it ("and then").

## How to make it
- Like the casual ending + 서: 가다 → 가서, 먹다 → 먹어서, 하다 → 해서, 이다 → 이라서 / 여서
- No past tense inside: 늦어서 (not 늦었어서).

## When to use it
- Reason: 늦어서 미안해. — Sorry I'm late. (being late, so sorry)
- Sequence where the first step leads to the second: 친구를 만나서 영화를 봤어. — I met a friend and (with them) watched a movie.
- Fixed thanks and apologies: 와 줘서 고마워.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Reasons with commands or suggestions (let's, please): use -(으)니까. 비가 오니까 우산 가져가.
- Plain listing of actions with no link: -고.

## Nuance and register
Neutral; very common in speech.

## Common mistakes
- Adding past tense inside (했어서).
- Using it before a command.

=== ko.connective_go | and -고
## What it is
-고 joins clauses or describes doing things in a row: "and", "and then".

## How to make it
- Stem + 고: 먹고, 가고, 예쁘고, 이고

## When to use it
- Listing actions or qualities.
착하고 재미있어. — She's kind and fun.
- One after another.
밥 먹고 가자. — Let's eat and then go.
- With some verbs, the manner or state: 안경을 쓰고 있어 (wearing glasses).

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- When the first action leads into or causes the second: -아서/어서.
- Ongoing actions: -고 있다 (see that guide).

## Nuance and register
Neutral.

## Common mistakes
- 가고 친구를 만났어 for "went and met a friend (there)": use 가서.

=== ko.connective_jiman | but -지만
## What it is
-지만 means "but, although", joining two contrasting clauses.

## How to make it
- Stem + 지만: 좋지만, 갔지만, 학생이지만

## When to use it
- Contrast.
비싸지만 맛있어. — It's expensive but tasty.
- Polite softener before a request: 죄송하지만… — I'm sorry, but…

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Background rather than contrast ("…, and/but"): -는데 is more natural in speech.

## Nuance and register
Clear and slightly formal; speech often prefers -는데.

## Common mistakes
- Overusing it in speech where -는데 sounds more natural.

=== ko.connective_neunde | background / but -는데
## What it is
-는데 sets up background for what follows ("…, and / so / but…"), or ends a sentence softly, leaving something unsaid.

## How to make it
- Verbs: -는데 (가는데, 먹는데); adjectives: -(으)ㄴ데 (예쁜데, 좋은데); past: -았/었는데 (갔는데)
- 이다: 인데

## When to use it
- Background before a question or request.
배고픈데 뭐 먹을까? — I'm hungry, so what shall we eat?
- Soft contrast.
좋은데 비싸. — It's nice, but expensive.
- At the end, hinting at something unsaid.
나는 괜찮은데… — I'm fine, though…

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- A clear cause with a result: -아서/어서 or -(으)니까.

## Nuance and register
Very common in speech; ending with it sounds hesitant or hinting.

## Common mistakes
- Adjective form: 좋는데 (wrong) → 좋은데.

=== ko.connective_eumyeon | if / when -(으)면
## What it is
-(으)면 means "if" or "when (whenever)".

## How to make it
- Vowel stem: + 면 (가면, 하면); consonant stem: + 으면 (먹으면); ㄹ stem: + 면 (살면)
- Past "if": -았/었으면 (갔으면)
- -았/었으면 좋겠다: "I wish / I hope". 네가 왔으면 좋겠어. — I wish you'd come.

## When to use it
- Conditions.
비가 오면 안 갈래. — If it rains, I won't go.
- Whenever.
슬프면 이 노래를 들어. — When I'm sad, I listen to this song.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- "When" for a single past event: -(으)ㄹ 때 or -았/었을 때.

## Nuance and register
Neutral; -았/었으면 좋겠다 is a gentle way to say what you want.

## Common mistakes
- 먹면 instead of 먹으면.

=== ko.ending_jana | you know -잖아
## What it is
-잖아 reminds the listener of something they already know or should know: "you know…", "…, remember?", "come on, it's…".

## How to make it
- Stem + 잖아: 가잖아, 먹잖아, 했잖아; 이다: 이잖아 / 잖아 (친구잖아)
- Polite: -잖아요

## When to use it
- Reminding.
내가 말했잖아. — I told you, didn't I?
- Justifying, as if it's obvious.
배고프잖아. — (Because) I'm hungry, obviously.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- New information the listener can't know: plain ending.

## Nuance and register
Casual and familiar; can sound reproachful (내가 말했잖아!).

## Common mistakes
- Using it with people you're not close to.

=== ko.ending_geodeun | you see, … -거든
## What it is
-거든 gives a reason or background the listener didn't know: "…, you see", "because…".

## How to make it
- Stem + 거든: 바쁘거든, 갔거든; 이다: 이거든 / 거든
- Polite: -거든요

## When to use it
- Explaining.
오늘 못 가. 일이 있거든. — I can't go today. I've got work, you see.
- Setting up a story: 어제 친구를 만났거든, 근데… — So I met a friend yesterday, and…

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Reasons the listener already knows: -잖아.
- In the middle of a sentence, -거든 means "if" (가거든 연락해 — call when you get there), a separate, more formal use.

## Nuance and register
Casual and chatty.

## Common mistakes
- Using it for shared knowledge (-잖아).

=== ko.aux_go_sipda | want to -고 싶다
## What it is
-고 싶다 means "want to (do)". For someone else's wish: -고 싶어하다.

## How to make it
- Stem + 고 싶다: 가고 싶어, 보고 싶어
- 보고 싶다 also means "miss (someone)": 보고 싶어. — I miss you.

## When to use it
- Your wishes.
집에 가고 싶어. — I want to go home.
- Missing someone.
너무 보고 싶어. — I miss you so much.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Wanting a thing (a noun): 원하다 or noun + 이/가 갖고 싶다.
- Someone else's want: -고 싶어하다 (그는 가고 싶어해).

## Nuance and register
Neutral; 보고 싶어 is everywhere in songs.

## Common mistakes
- 그는 가고 싶어 (sounds like his inner voice) instead of 가고 싶어해.

=== ko.aux_go_itta | doing (right now) -고 있다
## What it is
-고 있다 means an action is in progress: "is doing".

## How to make it
- Stem + 고 있다: 먹고 있어, 기다리고 있어; past: -고 있었어

## When to use it
- Ongoing actions.
지금 뭐 하고 있어? — What are you doing right now?
- Continuing situations.
아직 기다리고 있어. — I'm still waiting.
- Wearing: 안경을 쓰고 있어. — He's wearing glasses.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- Resulting states (sitting, standing, open): -아/어 있다. 앉아 있어 — is seated.

## Nuance and register
Neutral; the plain present often does the same job in speech.

## Common mistakes
- 앉고 있어 for "is seated" (that's "is in the act of sitting down").

=== ko.future_geot_ida | will / going to -(으)ㄹ 거다
## What it is
-(으)ㄹ 거다 (casual -(으)ㄹ 거야, polite -(으)ㄹ 거예요) talks about the future, or a guess: "will, going to, probably".

## How to make it
- Vowel stem: ㄹ 거야 (갈 거야); consonant: 을 거야 (먹을 거야)
- Questions: 갈 거야? — Are you going to go?

## When to use it
- Plans and intentions.
내일 갈 거야. — I'm going tomorrow.
- Guesses.
괜찮을 거야. — It'll be okay.

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- A promise or offer to the listener: -(으)ㄹ게 (내가 할게 — I'll do it).
- Asking what the listener wants to do: -(으)ㄹ래 (뭐 먹을래?).

## Nuance and register
Neutral. In songs, 괜찮을 거야 and 기억할 거야 are common.

## Common mistakes
- Using 할 거야 for promises where 할게 fits.

=== ko.modifier_neun_eun_eul | describing a noun -는 / -(으)ㄴ / -(으)ㄹ
## What it is
These endings turn a verb or adjective into a describer placed before a noun, like a relative clause: "the X that…".

## How to make it
- Verbs, now: -는. 웃는 얼굴 — a smiling face
- Verbs, past: -(으)ㄴ. 어제 본 영화 — the movie (I) saw yesterday
- Verbs, future or unrealised: -(으)ㄹ. 할 일 — things to do
- Adjectives: -(으)ㄴ. 예쁜 꽃 — a pretty flower; 달콤한 미소 — a sweet smile
- 있다 / 없다: -는 (재미있는, 맛없는)

## When to use it
- Describing a noun with a whole clause.
네가 좋아하는 노래 — the song you like
- With 것 / 때 / 수 / 적: 먹을 때 (when eating), 본 적 있어 (have seen).

## When it seems to fit but doesn't
- The subject inside the clause takes 이/가, not 은/는: 내가 만든 노래.

## Nuance and register
Neutral; used everywhere.

## Common mistakes
- Adjective with -는 (예쁘는 → 예쁜).
- Mixing up 본 (saw) and 볼 (will see).
